Writing hand vs other hand

What We Did

In 30 seconds, we wrote 'I am amazing' with our dominant hand. Then we reset the clock to 30 more seconds and wrote the same sentence with the other hand. We compared the results when writing with both hands. We then compared our handwriting against our professor's.

Results

Nine students. The benchmark is Dr. Jacques's writing-hand count, kept separate because it is what the class is being compared against.

Test Mean 1 Mean 2 Difference p-value
One-sample (writing hand vs. benchmark) 6.33 8.00 −1.67 0.0011
Independent-samples (yes vs. no) 6.40 6.25 0.15 0.848
Paired-samples (writing hand vs. other hand) 6.33 2.56 3.78 0.0000065

The Three Write-Ups

One-sample t-test: The class mean was lower than the benchmark by 1.67 points. The p-value is below .05.

We compared each student's score against Dr. Jacques's benchmark of 8.

Independent-samples t-test: The yes group mean was higher than the no group mean by 0.15 points. The p-value is above .05.

Paired-samples t-test: The writing-hand mean was higher than the other-hand mean by 3.78 points. The p-value is below .05.

In Plain Words

Since 7 out of 9 students scored lower than the benchmark and none scored higher, the class is performing below Dr. Jacques's standard. The average student score is approximately 6.33, which is about 1.67 points below the benchmark of 8.

What I Would Do Differently

We only had 9 students. This is a very small group, so one or two off days can make the whole class look worse than it is. We see the 'what' scores but not the 'why'. We don't know why they scored a 6 instead of an 8. Additionally, we are judging everything based on one specific column (Writing hand). It doesn't capture their performance in other areas like 'other hand' or 'script'. Lastly, we know the class is below the benchmark, but we don't know if this is a permanent struggle or just a one-time result.

Which test would you use?

  1. Paired-samples t-test. “Before” and “again after” means the same patients are measured twice.
  2. Independent-samples t-test. “Students who commute” and “students who live on campus” are two separate groups.
  3. One-sample t-test. The county’s average is being compared with one fixed value: the national average of 7.5 pounds.
  4. Correlation (Pearson correlation). The question asks whether two numeric measures—smoking and mental distress—vary together across ZIP codes.
  5. Paired-samples t-test. “Each member” is measured in both January and June, so the measurements are paired.
  6. Independent-samples t-test. Wait times are compared between two separate groups: patients at two different hospitals.
  7. Chi-square test of independence. Both variables are categorical yes/no responses, and the question asks whether they are related.
